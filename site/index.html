Celestial position fix

Starfix

Works out where a night-sky photo was taken, the way a ship's navigator would. It recognises the star pattern on its own, measures every star to a fraction of a pixel, and uses the horizon, the bending of starlight by the atmosphere, and the exact time to pin down latitude and longitude. Everything runs on your device; your photo is never uploaded.

Tap along the horizon where sky meets land or sea, at least 2 points spread wide. Drag a point to adjust it while the magnifier shows the exact edge.

Latitude comes from the stars and horizon alone. Longitude needs the time and its UTC offset (including daylight saving): every 4 minutes of clock error moves the answer about 100 km east or west.

    Camera and level

      How it works. The page finds every point of light and measures its centre to a fraction of a pixel. It recognises the star pattern by matching the shapes of star triangles against a built-in catalog of 230 navigational stars, trying focal lengths until the whole field lines up. Then a single joint fit solves the camera, the direction of straight up and the height of the terrain on the horizon together. Starlight near the horizon is bent upward by the atmosphere by a known amount, so the low stars act as a second spirit level that doesn't care about hills. Where straight up points among the stars at the moment the photo was taken is your latitude and longitude.

      Trained models. Two small models, trained in Python on simulated night skies, run in your browser. A star detector (a neural network trained on 150,000 examples) sets aside hot pixels, noise, lights, trails and tree edges before the pattern is recognised. An accuracy model (gradient-boosted trees trained on 40,000 simulated photos run through this solver) turns the fit's diagnostics into the ± you see.

      Everything runs in your browser; your photo is never uploaded. Planet positions load from the open-source astronomy-engine library when available.
